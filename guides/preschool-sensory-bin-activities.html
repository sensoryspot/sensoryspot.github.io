5 Sensory Bin Activities for Preschool Learning

A sensory bin can become a flexible place to practice early learning skills while children scoop, search, sort, and move small items with purpose.

Author: Sensory SpOT Studio

Intended audience: Preschool teachers, occupational therapists, early childhood professionals, and families

Supplies to gather

  • A shallow bin or tray
  • A filler appropriate for the children and setting, such as shredded paper, pom-poms, fabric scraps, or another supervised material
  • Large letter, number, picture, or object cards
  • Small containers for sorting
  • Optional tools such as scoops, cups, tongs, or clothespins
  • A matching sensory bin mat or simple recording sheet

Safety note: Choose materials with the children’s ages, individual needs, allergies, mouthing behavior, and supervision level in mind. Small loose parts are not appropriate for every child or setting.

1. Search for letters

Hide uppercase or lowercase letter pieces in the filler. Invite the child to find one letter at a time, name it if appropriate, and place it on a matching alphabet mat. If naming every letter is too much, offer two possible matches and ask the child to compare their shapes.

This version brings together letter recognition, visual scanning, and the fine motor work of moving material out of the way. Keep the number of hidden letters small at first, then add more when the child is ready for a busier visual field.

2. Count and match

Place groups of counters, number cards, or small seasonal objects in the bin. The child can find a card, identify the numeral, and move that number of items onto a counting mat. Encourage one-to-one counting by touching or moving each item once.

For an easier variation, use numbers within five. For a greater challenge, mix several number cards together or ask the child to compare which group has more or fewer items.

3. Sort by one visible feature

Add items that differ by color, shape, size, or picture category. Place two or three containers beside the bin and name the sorting rule clearly: “Put the blue pieces here and the yellow pieces there.” Once the first rule feels familiar, change the rule and notice how the same objects can be organized in a new way.

Sorting works best when the difference is easy to see. Avoid asking children to manage several sorting rules at once unless that complexity is intentional and appropriate.

4. Try a visual scanning challenge

Choose one target picture, letter, or number and place several copies throughout the bin. Give the child a matching sample and ask them to find all of the same targets. Move from a less crowded bin to a more crowded one to adjust the visual demand.

You can also place targets from left to right across a mat after each find. That gives the child a clear finished space and makes it easier to see how many remain.

5. Add fine motor tools

Offer a scoop for transferring, tongs for picking up larger pieces, or clothespins for clipping cards around the edge of the bin. The tool should add an interesting way to participate—not become a barrier to the learning goal.

Let children use their fingers when a tool is frustrating or when direct touch is the point of the activity. A short round with a tool followed by free exploration can keep the experience manageable.

Adapt the activity

Make it simpler

  • Use fewer targets and a shallow layer of filler.
  • Keep matching choices visually distinct.
  • Model one complete turn before asking the child to try.
  • Allow pointing, handing an item to an adult, or placing it on a match instead of requiring a spoken answer.

Add a challenge

  • Mix uppercase and lowercase letters and match the pairs.
  • Ask the child to find a requested quantity instead of a numeral card.
  • Sort the same objects using a second rule.
  • Add a simple recording step after the hands-on activity.
