Thanksgiving Movement Activities for Kids

Short seasonal movement activities can offer a change of position and pace while children practice following directions, planning movements, and noticing where their bodies are in space.

Author: Sensory SpOT Studio

Intended audience: Teachers, occupational therapists, related-service professionals, and families

Supplies

  • An open area with clear boundaries
  • Optional yoga or movement prompt cards
  • Optional Thanksgiving food or picture cards for matching
  • Floor markers, tape, or spots when children need visible locations

Before beginning: Check the space, footwear, furniture, and group needs. Offer a seated or lower-intensity option and avoid presenting movement as a required way to “fix” a child’s behavior.

1. Turkey pose sequence

Choose three to five simple positions and arrange them in a predictable order. Demonstrate one pose at a time, then move through the sequence together. A visual card can reduce the amount of spoken information children need to remember.

Pause between poses to notice foot placement, arm position, and breathing without promising a particular regulatory outcome. The purpose is to explore movement, body awareness, balance, and following a sequence.

2. Thanksgiving food movement match

Place picture cards around the room and keep matching cards in a small stack. A child selects one card, travels to the matching picture using the chosen movement, and returns to the starting area. Try walking, side stepping, marching, or another safe movement that fits the space.

For a small group, give each child a turn as the matcher while the group performs the travel movement. For a larger group, create several stations to reduce waiting.

3. Harvest carry and place

Use lightweight pretend food, beanbags, or picture cards. Ask children to carry one item from a “field” to a “basket,” moving along a clearly marked route. Change the route rather than increasing the weight: step over a line, walk around a cone, or stop at a balance spot.

4. Thanksgiving movement story

Tell a short, original story with movement cues: reach up to pick an apple, crouch to gather vegetables, carry a basket, or stretch tall like a tree. Keep each cue concrete and leave enough time for children to complete it before continuing.

5. Copy-the-leader feast

One person chooses a movement and the group copies it for a few repetitions. Connect the motion to the theme if desired—stirring, reaching, carrying, or setting the table—without requiring pretend play from children who prefer a direct movement prompt.

Skills to notice

Body awareness

Use simple position words such as high, low, wide, together, forward, and behind. Give children time to look at a model and adjust their own position.

Motor planning

Keep the starting position and route visible. Break a multi-step movement into one action at a time, then combine steps when appropriate.

Balance and coordination

Offer a wall, chair, or adult hand as a stable support when suitable. A smaller range of movement can be just as meaningful as a large pose.

Adapt for classrooms and therapy sessions

  • Whole class: Use two or three movements beside desks with a visible start and finish.
  • Small group: Rotate through matching, balance, and movement-story stations.
  • Individual session: Let the child help choose the sequence and repeat favorite movements.
  • Seated option: Adapt prompts to arm reaches, trunk turns, foot taps, or seated marching.
  • Reduced visual load: Show one movement card at a time.
